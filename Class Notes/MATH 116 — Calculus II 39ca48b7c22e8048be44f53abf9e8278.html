Extra Notes in Notation

  • d = differential
  • dx = an infinitesimally small change
  • Δx = an actual finite change
  • Suppose y = x^2.
    • If x changes a little, then y also changes.
    • We write that tiny change as dy.
  • dy/dx: The change in y with respect to the change in x. Or how fast y changes as x changes. Resulting derivative when y is the function being differentiated.
  • d/dx take the derivative with respect to x. Instruction.
  • Integrals: dx tells you which variable you’re integrating with respect to. Read it as “integrate x^2 with respect to x.
  • ∫03​x2dx means add up lots of tiny rectangles, width dx, height x^2. So one rectangle has area x^2dx.
    • The integral says add infinitely many tiny areas together. That’s why you see Area = Area=∑(height)(width)=∑x2Δz becoming ∫x2 dx
    • The Δx from the Riemann sum becomes the infinitesimal width dx.

Substitution Rule

Definite Integral Substitution Rule

Choose u: Pick the "inside" or more complex part of the function (often inside a power, root, or exponent).

Find du: Take the derivative of u with respect to x, then write du/dt = u'. Multiply both sides by dt to give the differential relationship du = u’dx.

Isolate dt: Divide both sides by u’.

Substitute: Replace all x terms and dx parts completely with u and du. Adjust integration limits by converting using u.

Integrate: Solve the new, simpler integral using basic rules. Take antiderivative.

Substitute back: Replace u with the original x expression to finish

Section 6.3: Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Comparing Methods

  • Look at Your Equations First (The Easiest Route)
    • Most of the time, the way your equations are written tells you the best variable to use
      • Use dx if your equations look like y = something with x. This is usually the easiest because you don't have to change any algebra.
      • Use dy if your equations look like x = something with y, or if the region is bounded by horizontal lines like y = 2 and y = 5.
  • 2. Visualize Your "Slices" Think of your region as a loaf of bread that you need to slice into thin pieces.
    • Vertical Slices = Integrating with dx
      • Your slices are standing up straight.
      • The width of each slice is a tiny change in x (dx).
      • Move your eyes from left to right to find your numerical integration bounds (x = a to x = b).
    • Horizontal Slices = Integrating with dy
      • Your slices are lying down flat.
      • The thickness of each slice is a tiny change in y (dy).
      • Move your eyes from bottom to top to find your numerical integration bounds (y = c to y = d).
  • Connect the Slices to Your Rotation Axis

Section 6.4: Work

Type 1: Single Object with a Variable Force Use this for: Springs (Hooke's Law), particles moved by a known force formula, or gravity fields.

  • Step 1: Identify the position variable (x). Define where (x=0) is. For springs, (x=0) is always the natural, unstretched length. For objects moving away from an origin, (x=0) is the origin
  • Step 2: Find the Force Function (F(x).
    • If given explicitly, use it directly.
    • For springs, use Hooke's Law ((F(x) = kx). You will usually be given a reference force and distance to solve for the constant k. K = force/distance. Make sure your units are consistent (convert inches to feet, or cm to meters).
  • Step 3: Determine the limits of integration ((a) and (b)). Identify the exact starting position (a) and ending position (b) in terms of your variable (x).
  • Step 4: Set up and evaluate the integral. Plug your components into the work formula and solve.

Type 2: Slicing Problems (Cables, Chains, and Tank Pumping) Use this when different parts of the system travel different distances (e.g., the top of a heavy rope only moves 1 foot, but the bottom link must be hauled up 50 feet).

  • Step 1: Set up a coordinate axis. Choose an orientation.
    • Tip: For tanks, setting (x=0) at the top lip of the tank and letting (x) represent the depth downwards usually makes the algebra simplest.
  • Step 2: Isolate a tiny "sample slice" at position (x) with thickness (dx).
  • Step 3: Write an expression for the Volume or Mass of that slice.
    • For Cables/Chains: Mass of slice = (linear density) * dx
    • For Tanks: Volume of slice = (Cross-sectional Area at depth x) * dx
  • Step 4: Turn that mass into a Force (Weight).
    • Metric System: Multiply mass by gravity g (9.8 m/s^2) or use water density pg = 9800N/m^3
    • US Customary System: Imperial weight density is already a force (e.g., water is 62.5 lb/ft^3, so no gravity multiplication is needed.
  • Step 5: Write the Distance formula for that specific slice. Determine how far that slice at position (x) must travel to reach its destination (e.g., if (x) is depth from the top lip, the distance to the top is simply (x).
  • Step 6: Combine into the Work Integral. Work is int([Weight density * Area(x) dx] * Distance(x)
  • Step 7: Set the limits ((a) and (b)) to match the material. The bounds (a) and (b) must strictly span where the material actually is (e.g., if a 5-meter tank only contains 3 meters of water at the bottom, your bounds are from (x=2) to (x=5)).

Angled force: W = F*d*cos(theta) where theta is the angle b/t the force and displacement
